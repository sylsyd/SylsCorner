The poem

Read aloud
Long from home. Glaciers capping the hills
like false teeth. It’s not just the odd meat
we’re carving, clawed flippers and flightless
wings, or the long-churned distance to any news of home,
any first-born or failing parent. There
are other signs this place is foreign. The ship
converses with ice packed around it, groans
and squeaks, an occasional outraged crack.It takes a particular man for this, you know,
able to be short-sighted for months on end.
The air is constantly aluminum with snow,
and my mouth, too, tastes of metal. Salt
of iron seeping from my weakened gums.
Each morning, I pack drift around my tongue
to freeze the soft flesh holding my teeth.It all goes to slush—ground underneath
our tents, my mouth, the knack for conversation.
Walking west, five of us have fallen
to dangle alongside cliffs of ice, the thin crust
breaking into chasm easily, as if such sudden transformations
were to be expected and we’re the fools to be surprised.
Only a thin rope holds us to the surface. Hanging,
there’s nothing to do but stare at the blue contours of freeze
and tongue our loosening teeth, test the stringy roots
that hold them, wait for a tug from the ones left above.
aluminum = US spelling of aluminium · drift = blown snow · chasm = a deep crack in the ice · flightless wings = penguins

5 minActivity 1Class

Crack the code

What might each line mean? Take a guess, then tap to reveal.

White ice sitting on the hilltops looks like a mouth full of false teeth. Remember this, teeth come back again and again.
Seals (clawed flippers) and penguins (flightless wings). The explorers are killing and eating them to survive.
The air looks like dull grey metal, thick with falling snow. It even makes his mouth taste of metal.
His gums are bleeding (the salty, iron taste is blood). This is scurvy: a disease from having no fresh food for months.
They have fallen into a crevasse (a huge crack in the ice). One thin rope is all that stops them dropping to their deaths.

4 minActivity 2 · voiceClass

Meet the narrator

Who is speaking? Find the clues in the poem. Tap each one as we spot it.

Clues “you know” talks to us like a mate. “we’re carving”, “five of us” show he is in a team, not alone. “my mouth”, “my teeth” are first person. The present tense (“it all goes to slush”) makes it feel like it is happening now, with us right beside him.

Danger or discomfort?

Tap once for Danger (could kill him), tap again for Discomfort (just miserable), tap again to clear.

Danger = deadly Discomfort = grim but survivable
Suggested answer Danger: falling into a crevasse, only a thin rope holding them, the ice cracking underfoot.
Discomfort: metallic taste, far from home, the groaning ship (eerie, not deadly).
Tricky: bleeding gums and loosening teeth feel like discomfort, but they are signs of scurvy, which could kill him in the end. Great if you argued that.

Big question · notebook
  • The land looks like teeth at the start. By the end, whose teeth are actually falling out, and why does Bradfield link the frozen land to his rotting mouth?
Model answer The explorer’s own teeth are loosening because of scurvy. Bradfield ties the icy land to his failing body so the danger feels total: the place is slowly eating him. The hills looked like false teeth at the start; by the end his real teeth are being tugged out by cold and disease. Even the rescue rope, “a tug from the ones left above,” sounds like pulling a tooth.
6
4 minSupport boxClass

Two meanings in one line

It takes a particular man for this, you know,
able to be short-sighted for months on end.

“Short-sighted” means two things here. Can you find both?

Both meanings 1. Literally (his eyes): his eyesight is damaged, he cannot see far. Bright snow glare wrecks explorers’ eyes.

2. In his mind: to survive he must not look ahead. He can’t dwell on home, family or the future, only on getting through today. Being “short-sighted” on purpose keeps him going.

8 minActivity 3 · tone + intonation

Prepare the dramatic reading

Words we need Tone = the feeling in your voice (serious, calm, grim).   Intonation = the rise and fall of your voice as you speak.

Which tones fit? Tap the words that suit this poem. Careful, some do not belong.

The last three do not fit: a dying man in the ice is not cheerful, excited or silly.

Mark up the ending in your notebook using these symbols:

// pause WORD stress it ↓ voice falls (quiet) almost a whisper slow stretch it out
Example: “Only a thin rope // holds us to the surface.” pause after rope, stress thin, then go (quiet).
Stretch · Tableaux

Add three freeze-frames

Groups hold a frozen picture while one person reads. Suggested moments:

  • The ship trapped, the crew listening to the ice “groan and squeak”.
  • The instant five of them fall into the crevasse.
  • Hanging on the rope, tonguing loose teeth, waiting for the tug from above.

Progress Check

What makes a strong reading?

Peers rate each performance 1 to 5 (5 is best) on these two things. Aim for a 5.

Intonation a 5 =

  • Voice clearly rises and falls, never flat.
  • Slows down on the fall and the loose teeth.
  • Drops to a whisper on the final line.

Tone a 5 =

  • Sounds weary and grim, matching the poem.
  • Makes the class feel the cold and danger.
  • Never slips into cheerful or silly.

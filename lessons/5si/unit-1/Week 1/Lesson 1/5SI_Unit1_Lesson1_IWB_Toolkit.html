Original micro-fiction

The Last Bus

At 8:42, the rain erased the far end of the road. Nia stood alone beneath the bus shelter, watching water creep over the toes of her shoes. Her phone insisted that the final number 14 had left eleven minutes ago.

She refreshed the screen. Nothing changed.

Nia should have been home already. She had stayed late for the drama auditions, then told her mother that rehearsal had overrun. That was not exactly a lie. It simply left out the locked prop cupboard, Amara’s missing audition folder and the lead role that now belonged to Nia.

Headlights appeared through the rain. A bus rolled towards her without a route number. A square of cardboard leaned against the windscreen: LAST SERVICE.

The doors folded open. The driver did not look at her.

“Does this go to River Road?” Nia asked.

“It goes where you need to go,” he said.

The answer should have sent her back into the rain. Instead, thunder cracked above the shelter, and Nia stepped inside.

The bus was empty. Its seats gave off the faint smell of dust and oranges. Nia sat near the front, keeping the driver in view. When she checked her phone again, the time was still 8:42. There was no signal.

Above the aisle, the next-stop display flickered into life.

WHAT YOU LEFT BEHIND

Nia stood. “I think I’m on the wrong bus.”

The driver watched her in the mirror. “People usually are.”

She pressed the stop button. No bell sounded. Outside, the shops had vanished. Rain streamed across the windows, but behind it there was only darkness.

The display changed.

THE ANSWER YOU DIDN’T GIVE

Nia saw Amara searching beneath the stage, asking everyone if they had seen her blue folder. She heard herself say no. One small word, delivered perfectly.

“I hid it,” Nia whispered.

The brakes shrieked. The bus stopped so suddenly that she grabbed a pole to stay upright. Its doors opened onto the school gates.

The rain had not yet begun. Her phone glowed: 7:51. Through the glass doors, Amara crossed the entrance hall carrying the blue folder towards the prop cupboard.

Nia turned back. The driver had finally looked at her.

“Last service,” he said, and closed the doors.

The bus pulled away. There was still no number on the back.

This time, Nia ran towards the building.

One-sentence gist

What difficult choice sits at the centre of the story?

Do not list every event. Name the character, pressure and key change.
A supernatural bus forces Nia to face the lie that won her a role and returns her to the moment when she can choose differently.
Sample three answers. If students only retell the journey, ask: “Why this journey, for this character, tonight?”

Useful or decorative?

Annotation must preserve thought

Her phone insisted that the final number 14 had left eleven minutes ago.
Weak:
Underline “phone” and write important.

It names no idea and explains nothing.
Useful:
Underline “insisted” and write certainty — later bus impossible.

It captures an inference and the reason.
Cover the mark. Read only the margin note. Does it still communicate thinking?
Select, do not decorate

Choose the three most useful annotations

Underline rain — weather
Underline insisted — certainty makes the later bus seem impossible
Box LAST SERVICE — sounds final and removes other choices
! beside the display — important
? Is the bus punishing Nia or giving her a second chance?
Underline blue folder — blue
Accept a different ranking only if the student can explain how the annotation records meaningful thinking.
Independent baseline

What does the writer make us expect?

Precise claim+Short evidence+How it guides the reader
Start with your own claim. If stuck: The writer initially makes us expect ___ because ___.
The writer initially makes us expect that the arriving bus is dangerous or supernatural. Its route panel is blank and the driver says it goes “where you need to go”, an answer that avoids the normal destination completely. This turns an ordinary journey into something uncertain and suggests that Nia may be trapped rather than taken home. By the ending, however, the danger becomes a second chance, so the reader’s expectation shifts from punishment to possible repair.
Do not reveal the model until pencils are down. This is baseline evidence, not a copying exercise.
